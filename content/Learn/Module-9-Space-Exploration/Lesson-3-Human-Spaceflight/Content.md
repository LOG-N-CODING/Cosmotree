# Lesson 3: Human Spaceflight

Human spaceflight presents extraordinary challenges that push the boundaries of life support technology and human endurance. Unlike robotic missions, crewed spacecraft must sustain complex biological systems in the harsh environment of space, where radiation, microgravity, and isolation create unprecedented physiological and psychological stresses.

Life support systems represent the most critical technology for human survival in space. Oxygen generation through electrolysis and sophisticated recycling systems maintain breathable atmosphere, while carbon dioxide scrubbers prevent toxic buildup. Water recycling achieves remarkable efficiency on the International Space Station, where even urine is processed into drinking water. Food production for long-duration missions remains a significant challenge, requiring innovative growing techniques and preservation methods.

Radiation protection poses severe health risks, as cosmic rays and solar particle events bombard spacecraft with dangerous energy levels. Effective shielding requires substantial mass, creating engineering trade-offs between protection and mission constraints. Biological effects include increased cancer risk and potential acute radiation syndrome during major solar storms.

Microgravity profoundly affects human physiology, causing bone density loss, muscle atrophy, and cardiovascular deconditioning. Astronauts experience fluid shifts that create facial puffiness and congestion. Countermeasures include rigorous exercise regimens and specialized equipment to maintain physical condition during extended missions.

The International Space Station exemplifies successful international cooperation, with fifteen nations contributing to this remarkable orbital laboratory that has hosted continuous human presence for over two decades.
